Progniños: Cuenca forecasts tested against real data

A first test using the Open-Meteo APIs, the Cuenca airport weather station and 1991–2020 climate data. Every number on this page comes from live API output.

G. The answer
CONDITIONAL YES

Automation works and is cheap. The script downloads the data, matches the dates, computes the differences and produces the chart and the English report in a few seconds. It can be rerun for any date.

The problem is the data itself, specifically rainfall. Open-Meteo's "past weather" is a model estimate, not a measurement. For Cuenca, the models show 1,800–2,000 mm of rain a year. Published figures for the city are around 800–900 mm, so the models show roughly twice the real rainfall. Temperature is in better shape: real airport measurements can be collected automatically.

The condition: until we find real rain-gauge data (INAMHI, ETAPA or Universidad de Cuenca), daily and weekly rainfall should be shown to readers in relative terms ("wetter or drier than normal"), not in millimetres.

Good news from a second test: CHIRPS, a free satellite-plus-rain-gauge dataset, gives realistic monthly rainfall for Cuenca (888 mm a year on average for 1991–2020). It can answer "Was last month wetter than normal?" in real millimetres, which is the question that matters most for El Niño coverage.

Main technical problems (F)

  • Comparing a model with a model. When the forecast and the "past weather" come from the same model family, the scores look artificially good. Daily highs differ by 0.5 °C against the model but 2.6 °C against the airport. Rain / no-rain days were right on 7 of 7 days against the model but only 4 of 7 against airport reports.
  • Rain amounts are unreliable. The models show rain in Cuenca almost every day. On Oct 23–25 they showed 6–12 mm a day; the airport reported no rain on those days.
  • Forecasts run consistently cool. Forecast daily highs were 2.6 °C below the airport readings on average (ERA5 averaged -1.3 °C over 2021–2025). Because the error is consistent, it can be corrected later.
  • Chance of rain is always 96–99%. The probabilities for the next 3 days tell readers nothing, and phrases like "heavy rain" could look alarmist. That runs against the whole idea of the project.
  • Two different data sources. The last week comes from IFS and the normal from ERA5, so the two are not fully comparable.

How this connects to El Niño

This first test checks day-to-day weather, and El Niño is not in it directly. The link is the question readers will ask over the coming months: "Is El Niño actually making Cuenca wetter?"

  • The question Progniños is built to answer. El Niño is an ocean pattern that plays out over months. Pacific forecasts don't tell us how much rain falls in Cuenca, which sits high in the Andes. What we can measure is whether the weeks and months here are actually wetter or drier than normal. That is section C, "Compared with normal", and it is the honest answer to alarmist El Niño headlines.
  • Why the rainfall problem matters most here. If the models already show twice the real rainfall, then "much wetter than normal" may simply be model error. That is the claim we must never get wrong while El Niño is in the news. Real rain-gauge data is the key to this question.
  • An easy addition. NOAA (CPC) and Columbia University's IRI publish regular El Niño bulletins. A small "El Niño status" box can be pulled from them automatically and shown next to the local data. The bulletin covers the ocean; the local data shows what is happening in Cuenca. Together they work against both alarm and complacency.

C. Compared with normal

Model-estimated rain this week: 49.4 mm, against a 1991–2020 average of 32.4 mm for the same dates (middle half of years: 20.0–42.8 mm). By the models' own yardstick, this week was wetter than usual, wetter than 26 of 30 reference years. Models overstate Cuenca rainfall, so read this as relative, not exact.

D. Forecast accuracy

  • Daytime highs: forecasts were 2.6 °C too cool on average, compared with the airport.
  • Rain or no rain: right on 4 of 7 days, compared with airport reports.
  • Seven days is too short to judge a forecast; scores become meaningful after several months.

E. Data sources

Forecasts: Open-Meteo Forecast and Previous Runs APIs. Past weather: Open-Meteo Historical Weather API (ECMWF IFS). Normals: ERA5, Copernicus Climate Change Service. Airport: METAR via Iowa Environmental Mesonet. Weather data by Open-Meteo.com (CC BY 4.0).

Licensing and cost (E)

  • The free plan is not an option. Open-Meteo's terms explicitly treat websites that show ads as commercial.
  • A Professional plan is needed. According to the pricing page, the Historical Weather and Ensemble APIs require Professional or higher, and Previous Runs is listed in the same group. The Standard plan covers only the live forecast.
  • Price: the pricing page itself shows no figures. Open-Meteo's own blog and third-party listings give Standard about $29 a month and Professional about $99 a month. Not confirmed: check the currency and VAT in the customer portal.
  • Free route: MET Norway (forecast) + our own archive (past forecasts) + airport METAR (temperature) + CHIRPS (monthly rain and normals) + GitHub Actions (daily run) = $0 a month. The trade-off is more sources to maintain and no chance-of-rain figure.
  • Recommended: Open-Meteo Standard (about $29 a month) for a simple, reliable forecast including chance of rain, plus the free sources for everything else. Professional is not needed. It is also worth asking Open-Meteo about a discount for local media.
  • Usage is very low: about 5 calls a day, about 150 a month. Even the smallest plan is far more than enough.
  • Cheaper alternative: our own forecast archive can replace Previous Runs. If real measurements come from a station, we don't need the Historical API either, and the Standard plan would be enough. The climate normals can be a one-time download; we should ask Open-Meteo whether that needs a commercial licence.
  • Attribution: CC BY 4.0. A "Weather data by Open-Meteo.com" link wherever the data appears, plus Copernicus attribution for ERA5.
  • Server: a scheduled job that runs once a day. No extra cost on an existing server, or a few dollars a month.

The code (D) and how it works

progninos.py uses only the Python standard library, with no extra packages. On every run it:

  • Adds today's forecast to its own archive (archive/forecast_log.csv).
  • Fetches the forecasts made 24 h earlier for the last 7 days, the past weather and the airport observations, and computes daily totals in local time.
  • Computes the differences and accuracy scores, and produces a chart (SVG), a table (CSV) and the English report (HTML).
  • Runs automatic checks: missing days, implausible values, grid point and time zone match, whether station data is present. If a check fails, the report is marked "needs human review".
  • Was rerun and tested for an earlier date with --date 2026-10-20.

These findings are based on 7- and 14-day samples. The size of the rainfall and temperature errors should be measured again over a longer period. Cuenca's annual rainfall figures come from secondary sources (about 790–920 mm); INAMHI's official normal has not yet been verified.
